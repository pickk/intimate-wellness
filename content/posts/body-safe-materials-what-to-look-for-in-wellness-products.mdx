When you buy anything that contacts your body, material safety isn't a feature — it's a baseline. Yet listings are full of vague descriptors like "body-safe," "skin-friendly," or "phthalate-free" without certification to back them up. This guide gives you a working framework for evaluating materials, the shortlist of safe options, and the red flags that should send you elsewhere.

## Why porosity is the defining property

The most useful single concept is **porosity** — whether a material has microscopic pores that can trap moisture, bacteria, and residue. Porosity dictates how thoroughly a product can be cleaned, which in turn dictates how safe it is over time.

- **Non-porous materials** can be fully sanitized. With proper care they do not harbor bacteria between uses.
- **Porous materials** can be surface-cleaned but never fully sterilized. Over time they degrade, retain odor, and pose a growing hygiene risk.

If you remember nothing else from this guide, remember this: porosity is the property that separates a product you keep for years from one you retire early.

## The body-safe shortlist

A small set of materials dominate the safe end of the market. Each has trade-offs, but all meet the non-porous threshold.

### Medical-grade silicone

The gold standard for flexible products. Non-porous, hypoallergenic, durable, and stable across a wide temperature range. It's easy to clean with mild soap and warm water, and tolerant of boiling for sanitization on most grades. The one caveat: silicone can degrade on contact with silicone-based lubricants. Pair it with water-based lubes to avoid damage.

### ABS plastic

Rigid, non-porous, affordable, and reliable. ABS lacks the softness of silicone but excels for any product where a firm surface is desirable. It's chemically stable and easy to sanitize, making it a workhorse material for budget-conscious buyers who still want safety.

### Borosilicate glass

Non-porous, inert, and temperature-stable. Glass is easy to sterilize and visually inspect for damage. The obvious trade-off is fragility — inspect for chips or cracks before every use, and retire any piece that has taken an impact.

## Materials to approach with caution

### Thermoplastic elastomer (TPE)

Soft and skin-like, which makes it appealing, but porous. TPE requires meticulous cleaning, thorough drying, and storage with renewing powder to remain usable. It also has a shorter service life than the materials above.

### "Jelly" and unnamed blends

If a listing says "jelly," "realistic feel," or simply "silicone blend" without further specification, treat it as porous and of unknown composition. Phthalates — plasticizers linked to health concerns — historically appear in this category. Avoid unless the manufacturer publishes a material certificate.

## How to verify claims

Marketing language is not a specification. Reputable manufacturers publish material data, and you can ask for it.

```text
Checklist before purchase:
1. Material named explicitly (e.g., "medical-grade silicone," not just "silicone")
2. Phthalate-free stated and certified, not implied
3. Cleaning and lube compatibility instructions provided
4. Manufacturer name and contact info visible
5. Independent reviews mention material feel and durability
```

If a product fails more than one of these checks, the safer move is to keep looking.

## A simple decision rule

When in doubt, default to the shortlist: silicone for flexibility, ABS for rigidity, glass for temperature play. Each is non-porous, well-documented, and easy to care for. The few dollars saved by choosing an unnamed blend rarely justify the hygiene and durability trade-offs. Material safety is the one spec where it pays to be boring.
